You have some money left over each month. You could throw it at your credit card,
or you could put it in the savings account earmarked for a house. Both feel
responsible. Only one of them gets you into a house sooner, and which one depends
almost entirely on a single number.

## The short version

Compare two percentages:

- The **interest rate on your debt**
- The **interest rate your savings earn**

If your debt costs more than your savings earn, paying the debt down first wins.
If they are close, it barely matters and you should pick whichever one you will
actually stick with.

That is the whole rule. The rest of this explains why, and shows what the gap
looks like in real dollars.

## Why paying debt early helps your down payment

This part is not obvious, so it is worth spelling out.

When your debt is gone, the money you were sending to it every month does not
disappear. It becomes free. If you were paying $1,000 a month toward a card and
you clear it in year two, that $1,000 a month spends the next three years going
into your house fund instead.

So paying debt aggressively does two things at once: it stops the interest, and
it frees up a monthly payment that then has years to compound in savings. That
second effect is the one people miss.

## What it looks like with expensive debt

Take someone with **$40,000 of debt at 22%** — a realistic credit card rate. Their
minimum payment is $1,000 a month, and they have another $2,000 a month they can
split however they like. Here is where they stand after five years, counting any
debt still owed against them:

| Extra toward debt | Debt gone in | Down payment after 5 years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0 | never | $118,335 |
| $500 | 37 months | $132,767 |
| $1,000 | 26 months | $135,867 |
| $1,500 | 20 months | $137,700 |
| $2,000 | 16 months | $139,698 |

Sending everything at the debt leaves them **about $21,000 better off** than
putting it all in savings. And look at that first row: with nothing extra going
to the debt, it never gets paid off at all. The balance grows faster than the
minimum payment can knock it down.

> A savings account paying 3% cannot outrun a credit card charging 22%. It is not
> close, and no amount of discipline changes the arithmetic.

## What it looks like with cheap debt

Now the same person, same everything, except the debt is at **3%** — a subsidised
student loan, a family loan, a promotional rate:

| Extra toward debt | Debt gone in | Down payment after 5 years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0 | 43 months | $147,004 |
| $500 | 28 months | $147,245 |
| $1,000 | 21 months | $146,835 |
| $1,500 | 17 months | $146,029 |
| $2,000 | 14 months | $146,420 |

The spread between best and worst is about **$1,200 on a $147,000 balance** — under
one percent. Financially these are the same decision.

So if being debt-free would let you sleep at night, do that. If you would rather
watch the house fund grow, do that instead. Neither one is the wrong answer, and
anyone who tells you there is a single correct choice here is overselling a
rounding error.

## The catch nobody mentions

There is a version of this that looks fine on a spreadsheet and fails in real
life: **paying so much toward debt that you have no cash buffer.**

If clearing the balance in 16 months means one car repair puts you straight back
on the card, you have not made progress. You have taken a lap. Keep enough set
aside that an ordinary bad month does not undo a year of work, and then throw the
rest at whichever side the numbers favour.
